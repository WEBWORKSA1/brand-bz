Brand Colors: Psychology, Palettes and the 60-30-10 Rule

By the Brand.bz Editorial Team · 9 min read · Updated November 06, 2026

Color is often the first thing people notice and the last thing they forget. Done well, a palette makes your brand recognizable in a fraction of a second. Done poorly, it creates noise, hurts readability and makes your marketing look accidental.

How color associations work

Colors do not carry fixed meanings — culture, context and competitors all matter — but there are broad associations worth knowing:

ColorCommon associationsWatch out for
BlueTrust, calm, reliabilityCrowded in finance and tech
GreenGrowth, health, nature, moneyCan feel generic in wellness
RedEnergy, urgency, passionCan signal danger or errors
OrangeFriendly, affordable, playfulMay read as cheap if overused
YellowOptimism, attentionPoor contrast on white
PurpleCreativity, premium, imaginationVaries widely by culture
BlackLuxury, sophistication, authorityCan feel cold without warm accents

Build a palette with roles, not just colors

A working brand palette assigns every color a job:

  • Primary — your signature color, used for the logo and the most important actions.
  • Secondary — supports the primary and adds depth.
  • Accent — used sparingly to highlight.
  • Neutrals — light backgrounds and dark text colors that carry most of the layout.

The 60-30-10 rule

Borrowed from interior design, the rule suggests about 60% of a layout uses a dominant neutral or base color, 30% uses a secondary color and 10% uses an accent. It prevents the "everything is shouting" effect and keeps your call-to-action buttons visible.

Harmony models

  • Analogous: neighboring hues — calm and cohesive.
  • Complementary: opposite hues — high energy and contrast.
  • Triadic: three evenly spaced hues — vibrant and balanced.
  • Monochrome: one hue in different lightness levels — minimal and elegant.

Accessibility is not optional

Text should meet a contrast ratio of at least 4.5:1 against its background for normal body copy (WCAG AA). Large headlines can use 3:1. Our palette tool shows whether each swatch passes with white or dark text. Accessible brands reach more people and generally convert better because content is easier to read.
