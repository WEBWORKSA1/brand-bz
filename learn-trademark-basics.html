Trademark Basics for New Brands (Plain-English Guide)

By the Brand.bz Editorial Team · 8 min read · Updated November 06, 2026

This guide is general information, not legal advice. Laws differ by country; consult a qualified trademark lawyer or agent for your situation.

A trademark protects the names, logos and slogans that identify the source of goods or services. For a new brand, understanding the basics can prevent a painful forced rebrand after you have invested in signage, packaging and marketing.

What a trademark does — and does not — protect

  • Protects: brand names, logos, taglines, and sometimes distinctive colors, sounds or packaging, when they identify the source of a product or service.
  • Does not protect: general ideas, common descriptive words used in their ordinary meaning, or business methods. Copyright covers creative works; patents cover inventions.

The distinctiveness spectrum

Marks are often described on a spectrum from weakest to strongest: generic (the common name of the product — not protectable), descriptive (describes a quality — hard to protect without proof of recognition), suggestive (hints at a benefit), arbitrary (a real word unrelated to the product) and fanciful (an invented word). The further right you go, the easier protection generally becomes.

Searching before you commit

Run free preliminary searches in the official databases:

  • United States — USPTO trademark search
  • Canada — Canadian Intellectual Property Office (CIPO)
  • European Union — EUIPO eSearch
  • International — WIPO Global Brand Database

Search exact spellings, phonetic variants and similar meanings within the classes of goods and services you plan to offer. Our Name Strength Checker generates direct search links for any name.

Registration in brief

Most countries use the Nice Classification system of 45 classes. You file an application listing the goods or services, an examiner reviews it, it is published for opposition, and if all goes well it registers. Timelines commonly range from several months to over a year. International protection can be extended through the Madrid System administered by WIPO.

Using the symbols

™ (or ℠ for services) is generally used to claim rights in a mark that is not registered. ® is typically reserved for marks registered with the relevant national office; misuse can carry penalties in some jurisdictions.

When to bring in a professional

  • Before spending significantly on launch materials
  • When search results show similar marks in related classes
  • When expanding to new countries
  • If you receive a cease-and-desist letter
